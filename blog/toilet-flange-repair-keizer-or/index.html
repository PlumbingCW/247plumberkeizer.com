Toilet Flange Repair in Keizer, OR

The toilet flange holds the toilet to the floor and seals it to the drain. When it breaks, the toilet rocks and leaks.

By the Keizer 24/7 Plumbing team · Published November 13, 2026

What the flange does

The flange is a ring that sits on the floor around the drain opening. The toilet bolts to it, and the wax ring seals between the toilet and the flange. If the flange cracks, rusts or sits too low, the toilet cannot sit firm and the seal fails.

You will not see the flange unless you lift the toilet. You will see the effects: rocking, a leak at the base, a sewer smell or stains around the bolts.

Signs of a bad flange

Watch for these.

Rocking toilet

Movement even after tightening the bolts.

Water around the base

A failed seal let water escape.

Sewer smell

Gas leaks past a poor seal.

Soft floor around the toilet

Water has rotted the subfloor.

Which one depends on the damage.

Flange kits are widely sold, but fit and floor condition decide success.

How the repair goes

A plumber can usually do it in one visit.

1

Pull the toilet

Shut off water, drain the tank and lift the toilet away.

2

Inspect and repair

Check the flange and floor, then fit a repair ring or replace the flange.

3

Reset

A new wax ring or seal goes on, the toilet is bolted down and tested.

Check the floor before you reset the toilet

A slow leak at the base can rot the subfloor. If the wood is soft, screws will not hold and a new flange will not stay tight. Probe around the flange with a screwdriver. Soft wood needs repair first.

If the leak has been long running, check for a hidden source too. Leak detection finds water supply leaks near the toilet, and toilet repair covers the rest of the toilet.

Getting the height right

When new flooring is added, the flange can end up below the surface. A flange sitting too low cannot compress the wax ring properly, so the seal leaks. An extension ring or spacer raises it to the right level. This matters especially after a tile remodel.

Common mistakes

Over-tightening the bolts is the classic error. Porcelain cracks, and then you are shopping for a new toilet. Tighten each nut a little at a time, alternating sides.

Another mistake is skipping the floor check. A new flange on soft wood will loosen again in a few months. Fix the floor first, even if it means a larger repair. A toilet that rocks again after a repair usually has a floor problem, not a flange problem.

When the problem is the pipe below the flange

Sometimes the flange is not the real problem. On an older cast iron drain, the pipe below the flange can be corroded or broken. A new flange cannot hold on a pipe that is crumbling. If the plumber sees rust scale or a crack below the floor, a larger repair is needed.

That repair may involve cutting out a section of the drain and replacing it. It is more work, but it is better done now than after a leak damages the floor and the ceiling below. A camera inspection can show the condition of the pipe further down the line.

Signs that the repair is bigger than a flange

Call a plumber if you see these.

Rust flakes around the opening

The pipe or flange is corroding.

A cracked pipe wall below

Visible once the toilet is lifted.

Water in the wall cavity below

The leak may be deeper than the seal.

Repeated leaks after resets

The base is not holding a seal.

What the code expects from a flange

The Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), expects a toilet to sit firmly on a flange that is secured to the floor and connected to the drain. A flange that is cracked, loose or sitting below the finished floor can let a toilet rock and leak.

Wax rings and flange repair kits come with manufacturer instructions, and those matter for the height and the seal. Rot in the floor around the flange is a structural issue and needs a proper repair first.

Resetting the toilet the right way

After the flange is fixed, set the toilet carefully. Place the new wax ring on the flange or on the toilet horn, whichever the product directs. Lower the toilet straight down onto the closet bolts without sliding it, because sliding smears the wax and breaks the seal.

Press down firmly, thread the washers and nuts and tighten them in small steps, alternating sides. Stop as soon as the toilet sits firm. Add the caps, reconnect the supply line and open the valve slowly. Flush several times and check the base and the supply for drips.

Finish with a bead of caulk around the base, if you like the look. Leave a small gap at the back, so a leak would show instead of hiding under the caulk. A toilet that stays dry for a week after the repair has a good seal.

Keizer notes

Keizer note: older floors, older flanges

The City of Keizer reports development in earnest from the 1950s onward, so many bathrooms have original floors and original flanges. Cast iron flanges rust, and a flange from the 1960s may need replacement rather than a ring.

If you are remodeling, ask the Keizer Building Department at (503) 856-3440 whether the work needs a permit. For Salem and Woodburn homes, contact the right office.
