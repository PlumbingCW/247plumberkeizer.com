Where to Install a Tankless Water Heater in Keizer

A tankless heater can go in more places than a tank, but each place has trade-offs in venting, gas supply and how long you wait for hot water.

Location comes before the model

Most people pick a unit first and ask where it goes later. It works better the other way around. The place you choose decides the vent route, the gas pipe length, the clearance from walls and the distance to your showers. Pick the place first, then size the unit to suit it.

A tankless heater is wall mounted and much smaller than a tank. That freedom is useful, but a vent and a gas line must still reach it. For the basics, see tankless water heaters.

Four common locations and their trade-offs

Each location suits a different house.

Garage wall

Easy venting and gas, room for service. Hot water travels farther to the bathrooms.

Utility closet

Short runs to fixtures. Needs proper clearance, combustion air and a vent path through the roof or wall.

Exterior wall

Frees interior space. Needs an outdoor-rated unit and freeze protection.

Basement or crawlspace

Good for distribution. Access and condensate drains need planning.

Venting decides a lot

Gas tankless heaters exhaust hot gases, and the vent has to be the right material, the right slope and the right length. Condensing models use plastic vent pipe and need a drain for condensate. Non-condensing models use metal vent and run hotter.

A shorter, straighter vent costs less and works better. A location that forces a long run through the attic can add significantly to the job. That is why we measure on site before quoting.

Distance to the fixtures and the wait for hot water

A tankless heater makes hot water on demand, but the water still has to travel from the heater to the tap. A long run to the far bathroom means a wait and a few gallons down the drain. A recirculation line or a small point-of-use unit can shorten the wait.

If your kitchen and master bath are at opposite ends of the house, that matters more than whether the heater sits in the garage or the closet.

Permits, code and clearances

New gas appliances need to follow the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code and the manufacturer’s installation instructions. A gas line change or a new vent usually needs a permit and an inspection. Ask your local building office before you start.

If you are also deciding between a tank and a tankless unit, water heater installation compares them.

A walk-through: garage or closet?

Picture a 1960s ranch with the old tank in a garage corner and the bathrooms at the far end of the house. Putting the tankless unit on the garage wall keeps the gas and the vent short, but hot water has a long trip to the bathroom. Moving it to a hall closet shortens the trip, but the vent now needs a path through the roof.

Neither answer is wrong. The right one depends on what you value, a lower install cost or a shorter wait at the tap. A plumber who walks the house with you can lay out both options with real numbers, and that conversation is worth more than any general rule.

Noise, access and future service

A tankless heater makes some sound when it fires, so avoid placing it against a bedroom wall. Leave room in front for service, because the unit needs a yearly flush and the isolation valves need to be reachable.

A unit that is easy to reach is serviced more often, and a unit that is serviced lasts longer. Thinking about the person who will flush it next year is a good test of any location.

Gas line sizing and why the location matters

A tankless unit can need several times the gas of a tank. The line from the meter to the heater must be large enough to deliver it without a pressure drop. A heater far from the meter needs a longer line, and a longer line needs a larger pipe to carry the same gas.

This is why a location near the existing gas supply saves money. If a plumber tells you the gas line needs upsizing, ask to see the calculation. The manufacturer publishes sizing charts based on the length of run and the number of fittings, and a good installer works from them rather than guessing.

Code and manufacturer rules that decide the location

The location is set by the manufacturer’s installation instructions and the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, which is the state’s adoption of the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). Clearances, vent length, combustion air and condensate drainage all follow those documents.

Electric units are wired under the National Electrical Code (NEC). A permit and an inspection are normal for a tankless install, so ask your plumber how they handle both.

Planning for the unit you do not have yet

Many homeowners choose a location for the heater they plan to buy next year, not the one they have now. If you may add a bathroom, plan for the extra demand. If you may switch to a heat pump unit later, leave room and a drain path.

A location that works for three options is better than a location that works for one. Ask your plumber to sketch what the space would look like with each type of heater. A rough drawing on the back of an envelope is enough to show whether a closet is realistic, and it prevents a purchase that does not fit.

Take photos of the current setup before the visit. The photos help a plumber who cannot see the space plan the vent route, the gas line and the clearances before arriving, which shortens the visit.
