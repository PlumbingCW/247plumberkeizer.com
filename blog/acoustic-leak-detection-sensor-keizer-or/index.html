Acoustic Leak Detection Sensors in Keizer

Water escaping from a pressurized pipe makes a sound. Acoustic sensors listen for it and help narrow down where the leak sits.

By the Keizer 24/7 Plumbing team · Published November 13, 2026

The principle: leaks make noise

When water escapes from a pipe under pressure, it creates vibration and sound. That noise travels along the pipe and through the ground or the building. An acoustic leak detector uses sensitive microphones and amplifiers to pick it up, even when a human ear cannot.

The technician listens at several points along the line. The place where the sound is loudest marks the likely leak position, which narrows the search to a small area.

Tools in a typical kit

No single tool finds every leak, so detection uses several.

Ground microphone

A sensitive mic placed on soil or concrete to hear buried leaks.

Contact sensors

Clip-on or probe sensors on pipes, valves and meters.

Correlator

Compares sound at two points to calculate the leak’s distance.

Thermal camera

Shows warm spots from hot water leaks. See leak detection.

A detection visit with sensors

The steps run in order from broad to narrow.

1

Isolate and test

Fixtures are shut off, the meter is read and the lines are pressure tested in sections.

2

Listen

The tech places sensors along exposed pipe, at valves and over the suspect run.

3

Pinpoint and mark

The loudest point is marked, confirmed with a second method and reported to you.

What acoustic cannot do

Sound sensors cannot find a leak in a drain pipe, because drains are not under pressure. A cracked sewer line is found with a camera instead. See sewer inspection.

Very slow leaks make little noise. For those, a pressure test or a long meter test can show that water is lost, and thermal imaging or tracer gas may help locate it.

What to do with the result

A marked location lets the repair be targeted. Instead of opening a whole wall or slab, only a small area needs to be exposed. That saves money and time. The final report also documents the leak for your insurance claim.

If the leak is on the supply line to the house, the fix may be a service line repair, and burst pipe repair covers failed lines inside.

Why the meter test comes first

Before any sensor goes on a pipe, the technician reads the meter with the house shut off. That tells them whether a leak exists, and roughly how big it is. A fast-moving dial points to a large leak that makes a lot of noise. A slow creep points to a small one that may need pressure testing.

Doing the meter test first also keeps the visit efficient. If the dial holds still, the technician does not waste time listening for a leak that is not there.

Honest limits and expectations

Acoustic detection is a skilled craft, and the results depend on the pipe, the soil and the background noise. A good technician tells you how confident they are and confirms with a second method before opening anything.

Be wary of anyone who promises to find every leak every time. The honest promise is a narrowed search area, a clear explanation and a repair plan that opens as little as possible.

Leak noise and what it tells the technician

A pinhole leak has a hissing sound. A larger break has a rushing sound at a lower pitch. The sound changes with pipe material, pressure and the soil or fill around the pipe. An experienced technician learns to tell a leak from other noise, such as a running toilet, a pump or traffic.

Because the sound travels along the pipe, the loudest point at a valve or fitting is not always the leak. That is why a correlator, which compares the arrival time of the sound at two sensors, is useful. It calculates where the sound started, and marks the point for confirmation.

What to do before the visit

A few steps make the search more accurate.

Know your shutoffs

Find the main valve and any zone valves.

Keep the house quiet

Avoid running appliances during the test.

Tell us about repairs

Recent work can explain new noises.

Note the meter reading

It gives us a starting number.

What the report should contain

A good detection report states what was tested, what was found and how confident the technician is. It lists the meter reading before and after, the sections of pipe that were isolated and the point or area where the leak is believed to be. It may include photos of the marked location and the sensor readings.

It should also state the limits. If a section could not be tested, the report should say so. If the signal was weak because of a slab or plastic pipe, that belongs in the notes. An honest report helps you and your insurer understand the result.

Keep the report with your home records. If you sell, a documented leak repair is a reassurance to a buyer. If you file a claim, the report is the evidence that supports the cost of the repair and the access work.
