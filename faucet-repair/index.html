What faucet repair covers

Cartridge, aerator and seat repairs, and replacement when parts are no longer available.

Tub and shower valves

Supply and angle stops

The small valves under sinks and behind toilets that seize or leak.

Outdoor hose bibs

Dripping or broken spigots, and the frost-proof type that leak inside the wall.

Why faucets drip and valves stick

Inside a modern faucet is a cartridge or a ceramic disc. Over time the rubber seals wear and mineral deposits scratch the surfaces, so water slips past even when the handle is closed. An older compression faucet uses a washer and seat that wear the same way.

Shutoff valves under sinks sit for years without turning. Their stems corrode and their packing dries out, so when you finally turn one it leaks or will not move. If a valve breaks, we replace it with a quarter-turn stop that is easier to use.

Showers and tubs

A tub spout that drips or a shower that runs on after you turn it off usually has a worn cartridge inside the wall. The repair is done from the handle side, and we only open the wall when the valve body itself is damaged.

A showerhead that sprays from the arm can be a loose connection or a worn seal. If water appears on a ceiling below the bathroom, call at once. That may be a hidden leak, and leak detection finds it.

Standards worth looking for on a faucet

Faucets sold in the United States are made to published standards. ASME A112.18.1 and CSA B125.1 cover plumbing supply fittings, and NSF/ANSI 61 covers parts that touch drinking water. A faucet marked to those standards is a safer buy than an unmarked one.

Replacement installs follow the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code and the maker’s instructions. Shutoff valves under the sink should work before you start. If they do not, replacing them is part of the job, not an extra.
